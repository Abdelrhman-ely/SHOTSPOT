The problem

At the lunar south pole, a few meters decide everything.

The south pole is where the next landers want to go: it may hold water ice, and some high ground sees sunlight for long stretches. But it is also the hardest place on the Moon to land well.

The Sun barely rises

Near the pole the Sun circles the sky only a few degrees above the horizon. A ridge kilometers away can hide it for days: no sunlight means no power, and the lander freezes.

Earth slips below the horizon

Earth also sits low in the polar sky. When terrain hides it, there is no direct radio link with the ground stations, so the team cannot talk to the lander.

Landers miss their mark

No lander touches down exactly on its aim point. In 2025 the Athena lander came down hundreds of meters from its target. A perfect spot is useless if the spots around it are in shadow or on a slope.

“If I aim here and the lander may drift up to X meters, what share of the spots I might hit still get enough sunlight and Earth contact, and where should I aim instead?”

SHOTSPOT answers this question with real NASA terrain and ephemeris data.

Honesty

Honest about its limits

  • Solar visibility, not power. A body counts as visible when its center is above the terrain horizon.
  • Geometric Earth visibility, not link quality. No antennas or relays are modeled.
  • Drift is your input. A circular, uniform disk around the aim point, not a prediction.
  • Small hazards are invisible. Rocks and craters under ~10 m do not show at 5 m/px.
